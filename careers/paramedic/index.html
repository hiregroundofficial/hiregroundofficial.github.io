Paramedic

Paramedics are the first medical responders to emergencies, they rush to 911 calls, treat patients at the scene, and transport them safely to hospital. About 11,600 people work in paramedical occupations in Ontario, mostly for ambulance services, and 92% work full-time. Ontario has three levels: Primary Care (PCP), Advanced Care (ACP), and Critical Care (CCP) paramedic.

What does a Paramedic do?

Ambulances, emergency scenes (homes, roadways, public places), and hospital emergency departments. Work is fast-paced, physically demanding, and done in all weather and unpredictable conditions.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Senior biology, chemistry, and English. Programs are very competitive, high grades help. First aid/CPR and volunteer work in emergency or health settings are valuable.

College Diploma

Complete a 2-year Primary Care Paramedic (PCP) college diploma, which includes theory, lab/clinical practice, and a land-ambulance field placement.

Provincial Exam

Pass the Advanced Emergency Medical Care Assistant (A-EMCA) exam administered by the Ontario Ministry of Health.

Additional Requirements

Obtain a Class F driver's licence (required to operate ambulances), plus medical clearance and current CPR certification.

Entry-Level Position

Get hired by a certified land ambulance service and work as a Primary Care Paramedic responding to emergency calls.

Career Progression

Advance to Advanced Care Paramedic (graduate certificate program) and then Critical Care Paramedic, or move into supervision, dispatch, or education roles.

Licensing: In Ontario, paramedics must pass the provincial Advanced Emergency Medical Care Assistant (A-EMCA) exam and be employed through a certified land ambulance service. Paramedics are not self-regulated; the Ontario Ministry of Health sets the requirements.

Job demand

Good

Stress level

High

High stress from responding to life-threatening emergencies, exposure to trauma and death, shift work with irregular and long hours, unpredictable calls, and physically demanding rescue work.

Work-life balance

Challenging

12-hour shifts, nights, weekends, and holidays are standard. The schedule is demanding, though compressed schedules (e.g., 4-on/4-off) give blocks of time off.

Flexibility

  • Remote workNone, emergency response is entirely on-site.
  • SchedulingFixed rotating shift schedules with limited personal control.
  • FreelanceVery limited, less than 5% are self-employed. Most work as employees of ambulance services.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Respond to 911 emergency calls by ambulance
  • Assess patients at the scene and prioritize treatment
  • Provide emergency care: oxygen, defibrillation, IVs, medications
  • Stabilize trauma patients and manage airways
  • Transport patients safely to hospital
  • Communicate patient condition to hospital staff
  • Complete patient care reports and documentation
  • Check, clean, and restock ambulance equipment

Where it can lead

  • Primary Care Paramedic (PCP)
  • Advanced Care Paramedic (ACP)
  • Critical Care Paramedic (CCP)
  • Superintendent, supervisor, or operations manager
  • Educator, dispatch, or emergency management roles
