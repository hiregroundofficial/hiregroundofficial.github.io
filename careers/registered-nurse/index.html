Registered Nurse

Registered nurses provide direct medical care to patients, from giving medications and monitoring vital signs to creating care plans and teaching patients about their health. They work in hospitals, clinics, long-term care homes, and community settings, and are the largest group of health care workers in Canada. About 134,400 people work in this occupation in Ontario.

What does a Registered Nurse do?

Mostly hospitals (71% in Ontario), plus clinics, long-term care homes, doctors' offices, and community agencies. Work is physically active, involves shift schedules, and requires constant collaboration with doctors and other health workers.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Take senior-level biology, chemistry, and English. Strong grades matter, BScN programs are competitive. Volunteer or work in health care settings (e.g., hospital volunteer, long-term care) to confirm your interest.

Postsecondary Education

Complete a 4-year Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BScN) from an approved university nursing program (many offered as college–university collaborations).

Licensing Exams

Pass the NCLEX-RN registration exam and the RN Jurisprudence Examination, and meet CNO registration requirements (nursing education, language proficiency, police record check, good character).

Entry-Level Position

Register with the College of Nurses of Ontario (CNO) and start as a staff nurse in a hospital unit, clinic, long-term care home, or community setting.

Career Progression

Specialize (e.g., emergency, ICU, mental health), become a charge nurse or manager, teach, or pursue a Master's degree to become a Nurse Practitioner (NP).

Stress level

High

High stress from heavy workloads, responsibility for patients' safety and lives, shift work including nights and weekends, emotionally difficult situations, and staffing shortages reported across Ontario hospitals.

Work-life balance

Challenging

Shift work (days, evenings, nights, weekends, holidays) makes scheduling unpredictable, though part-time and casual roles are widely available, which some nurses use to improve flexibility.

Flexibility

  • Remote workVery limited, almost all duties require in-person patient care. Telehealth triage roles exist but are a small niche.
  • SchedulingHospitals run 24/7 shifts; some community and clinic roles have regular daytime hours. Part-time work is common.
  • FreelanceAgency/travel nursing contracts are common. Private-duty and self-employed practice are possible but uncommon.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Administer medications, injections, and IV therapy
  • Monitor vital signs and changes in patient condition
  • Develop and update patient care plans
  • Educate patients and families about treatment and recovery
  • Document care in electronic patient charts
  • Collaborate with physicians and allied health professionals
  • Respond to medical emergencies and code calls
  • Prepare patients for procedures and surgeries

Where it can lead

  • Staff nurse in a hospital unit or clinic
  • Senior/charge nurse leading a shift
  • Specialized nurse (emergency, ICU, oncology, mental health)
  • Nurse educator, manager, or clinical nurse specialist
  • Nurse Practitioner (requires a master's degree)
