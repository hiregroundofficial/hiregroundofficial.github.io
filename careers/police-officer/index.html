Police Officer

Police officers protect the public by preventing crime, responding to emergencies, and enforcing the law. They patrol communities, answer 911 calls, investigate crimes, arrest suspects, write reports, and testify in court, working in all weather, day and night.

What does a Police Officer do?

On patrol in neighbourhoods and on highways, at police stations, and at incident scenes, indoors and outdoors, in all weather, day and night. Shift work including nights, weekends, and holidays is standard.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Earn your Ontario Secondary School Diploma. Stay physically fit, keep a clean record, and get involved in your community, volunteering and leadership experience strengthen your application.

OACP Certificate and First Aid

Complete the OACP Certificate of Results (written test, PREP fitness test, vision and hearing standards) during the recruitment process. Obtain Standard First Aid and CPR Level C.

Apply and Pass Screening

Apply to a police service. You must be a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, at least 18, hold an OSSD or equivalent and a valid Class G licence, and pass interviews, medical/psychological assessments, and background/security checks.

Police College and Field Training

Successful recruits complete 13 weeks of Basic Constable Training at the Ontario Police College, then field training with a coach officer before patrolling independently.

Career Progression

Advance through constable classes, then specialize (K-9, traffic, forensics, community policing) or move into detective work and supervisory ranks, sergeant, staff sergeant, inspector, and beyond.

Education time

under 1 year after being hired
  • •High school: Ontario Secondary School Diploma (4 years)
  • •Recruitment process: written, fitness, medical, and background stages (several months)
  • •Training: 13 weeks of Basic Constable Training at the Ontario Police College, plus field training with a coach officer

Mandatory: The minimum requirements are OSSD, age 18+, citizenship/PR status, Class G licence, first aid/CPR, and passing all recruitment and training stages. No postsecondary credential is mandatory.

Optional: College or university education is optional but is an asset in competitive applicant pools.

Stress level

High

Officers face dangerous, unpredictable situations, violent confrontations, accidents, and emergencies, where decisions carry life-or-death consequences. Rotating shift work, exposure to trauma, and public scrutiny add to the strain.

Work-life balance

Challenging

Rotating shifts including nights, weekends, and holidays disrupt sleep and family routines. Overtime for court appearances, extended investigations, and emergencies is common.

Flexibility

  • Remote workNot possible, the work is in the community and at incident scenes.
  • SchedulingRotating shifts including nights, weekends, and holidays; overtime for court appearances and emergencies.
  • FreelanceNot applicable, policing is full-time public employment.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Patrol neighbourhoods by car, bike, or on foot to prevent crime
  • Respond to 911 calls and emergencies
  • Investigate crimes: gather evidence, interview witnesses and suspects
  • Arrest suspects and prepare case files for court
  • Testify in court as a witness
  • Write detailed reports on incidents and investigations
  • Engage with the community to build trust and solve local problems

Where it can lead

  • Police Cadet / Recruit
  • Probationary Constable (post–Police College)
  • 4th/3rd/2nd/1st Class Constable
  • Detective or Specialist Unit Officer
  • Sergeant, Staff Sergeant, and higher ranks
