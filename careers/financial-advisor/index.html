Financial Advisor

Financial advisors help individuals and families plan for their financial future, saving, investing, managing debt, and preparing for retirement or estate needs. They assess a client's goals and risk tolerance, then recommend products such as mutual funds, investments, and insurance, earning income through salary, fees, or commission. In Ontario the title is protected by law, so you must hold an FSRA-approved credential or registration to call yourself a Financial Advisor.

What does a Financial Advisor do?

Most financial advisors work in bank branches, investment firm offices, or insurance offices, meeting clients in person at the office or at client locations such as homes and businesses. Self-employed advisors may also work from a home office.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High school (grades 9–12)

Earn your Ontario Secondary School Diploma with strong grades in math (Grade 12 functions, advanced functions, or calculus), business studies, economics, and English.

Bachelor's degree (4 years)

Job Bank notes this occupation usually requires a university degree, typically a bachelor's in business, finance, economics, or commerce. Co-op terms or internships at banks and investment firms build experience and employer connections.

Licensing and proficiency courses

Complete industry courses required for the products you will sell: the Canadian Securities Course (CSC) and Conduct and Practices Handbook (CPH) for securities and mutual funds, or the Life Licence Qualification Program (LLQP) for insurance. Employers often sponsor these.

First advisor role and registration

Get hired by a bank, investment dealer, or insurer. Registration with CIRO (for securities/mutual funds) or licensing by FSRA (for insurance) is tied to your employer and legally required before you can sell regulated products.

Earn an approved credential for the title

In Ontario, using the 'Financial Advisor' title requires an FSRA-approved credential, such as the PFA, RFRA, or RRA designation, or registration with CIRO as a Registered Representative or Mutual Fund Dealing Representative. The CFP designation is the best-known planning credential (bachelor's degree, national exam, 3 years of experience) and permits the 'Financial Planner' title.

Ongoing: continuing education and client building

Maintain credentials through annual continuing education, grow your client book, and progress toward senior, private wealth, or management roles.

Required qualifications
  • ✓An FSRA-approved credential to legally use the title 'Financial Advisor' in Ontario (e.g., PFA, RFRA, or RRA designation, or CIRO registration as a Registered Representative or Mutual Fund Dealing Representative)
  • ✓Registration with CIRO through a registered dealer to sell securities or mutual funds
  • ✓A licence from FSRA to sell life or accident and sickness insurance in Ontario, as required by Ontario Regulation 347/04
  • ✓Industry proficiency courses for the products sold, e.g., the Canadian Securities Course (CSC) and Conduct and Practices Handbook (CPH) for securities, or the LLQP for insurance
  • ✓Employment or sponsorship by a registered dealer, insurer, or financial institution (registration and licences are tied to an employer)
Common qualifications
  • ○Bachelor's degree in business, finance, economics, or commerce (Job Bank: usually required)
  • ○Certified Financial Planner (CFP) or another planning designation (PFP, QAFP, CLU)
  • ○1–3 years of client-facing sales or customer-service experience
  • ○Strong sales, relationship-building, and communication skills
  • ○Clean background and suitability check for registration/licensing
Optional qualifications
  • +Chartered Financial Analyst (CFA) or Chartered Investment Manager (CIM) designation
  • +Graduate degree such as an MBA or Master of Finance
  • +Chartered Professional Accountant (CPA) for tax-planning specialization
  • +Additional designations such as CLU, PFP, or QAFP
  • +Bilingualism (English/French), an asset for Ontario clients
Licensing: In Ontario, licensing depends on what you sell: trading securities or mutual funds requires registration with CIRO through a dealer (with proficiency courses such as the CSC and CPH), while selling insurance requires a licence from FSRA under Ontario Regulation 347/04. Separately, Ontario's title-protection law requires an FSRA-approved credential (e.g., PFA, RFRA, RRA, or CIRO registration) to call yourself a 'Financial Advisor', the well-known CFP designation permits the 'Financial Planner' title instead.

Optional: Designations like CFP, PFA, or QAFP typically add 1–3 years of study and supervised experience beyond the degree.

Stress level

High

Advisors face sales targets and performance quotas, direct responsibility for clients' savings and retirement money, and income that can swing with markets and commission structures, pressure documented by Job Bank, which also flags major layoffs in the banking industry and a workforce that is 30% self-employed.

Work-life balance

Moderate

Most bank and firm advisors work standard business hours, but client meetings can stretch into evenings and weekends to accommodate clients' schedules, and sales pressure can spill into personal time. Self-employed advisors control their own hours but face the added stress of building and retaining their own client book.

Flexibility

  • Remote workMostly office-based at bank branches, investment firms, or insurance offices, with frequent in-person client meetings; hybrid options exist at some firms but the role is fundamentally client-facing.
  • SchedulingBank-employed advisors generally follow branch hours, sometimes with evening or weekend client appointments; self-employed advisors set their own schedules around clients.
  • FreelanceSelf-employment is common, 30% of Ontario financial advisors are self-employed, often working on commission, fee-based, or a mix of salary plus incentives, so income varies with sales.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Meet with clients to review financial goals, income, debts, and risk tolerance
  • Build personalized financial plans covering savings, investments, insurance, and retirement
  • Recommend suitable financial products such as mutual funds, investments, and insurance policies
  • Monitor client portfolios and adjust strategies as markets or life circumstances change
  • Prospect for new clients through referrals, networking, and community outreach
  • Prepare and maintain compliance documentation for every recommendation and transaction
  • Stay current on market trends, tax rules, and regulatory requirements
  • Complete annual continuing education to maintain licences and credentials

Where it can lead

  • Advisor trainee / associate advisor, learning products and shadowing senior advisors
  • Financial advisor, managing an own client book and hitting sales targets
  • Senior advisor / private wealth advisor, serving high-net-worth clients and larger portfolios
  • Branch manager / team lead, supervising and mentoring other advisors
  • Portfolio manager, firm owner, or independent practice, for experienced, licensed professionals
