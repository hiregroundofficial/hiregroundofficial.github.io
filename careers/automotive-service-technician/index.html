Automotive Service Technician

Automotive service technicians, often called mechanics, inspect, maintain, and repair cars and light trucks. They use computerized diagnostic tools to find problems, then fix or replace engines, brakes, transmissions, and electrical systems.

What does an Automotive Service Technician do?

Vehicle repair shops, car dealerships, and fleet maintenance garages. Hands-on work in a shop environment with lifts, tools, computers, engine noise, and occasional exposure to oils, solvents, and exhaust.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Earn your Ontario Secondary School Diploma with math, science, English, and automotive technology courses. OYAP or a co-op at a repair shop or dealership counts toward apprenticeship hours.

Find a Sponsor and Register

Get hired as an apprentice by a repair shop, dealership, or fleet operator willing to sponsor you, then register a Training Agreement with Skilled Trades Ontario.

Apprenticeship

Complete about 7,220 hours of combined training, roughly 6,500 on the job and 720 in school over three levels, typically about 4 years.

Certification

Pass the Certificate of Qualification exam for 310S Automotive Service Technician. This is a compulsory trade, the certificate is legally required to work unsupervised in Ontario.

Career Progression

Licensed technicians can become diagnostic specialists, service advisors, shop forepersons, or open their own repair shops. Electric-vehicle and hybrid specialization is a growing niche.

Stress level

Moderate

Technicians work on their feet all day in physically demanding positions, handling heavy parts and chemicals. Diagnostic puzzles and same-day repair deadlines create time pressure, and explaining expensive repairs to customers takes patience.

Work-life balance

Moderate

Typical shop hours are daytime with some evening or Saturday shifts; overtime occurs during busy periods. Work is steady year-round since vehicles always need repair.

Flexibility

  • Remote workNot possible, the work is hands-on with physical vehicles.
  • SchedulingRegular shop hours; some shops offer evening or Saturday shifts.
  • FreelancePossible, some licensed technicians run mobile repair businesses, though most are employees of shops or dealerships.

  • Learn to use OBD-II scanners and basic diagnostic software
  • Get a part-time job as a lube tech or tire tech to get started

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Inspect vehicles and listen to customers' descriptions of problems
  • Run computerized diagnostic tests to find faults
  • Repair or replace engines, transmissions, brakes, and suspensions
  • Perform routine maintenance: oil changes, tire rotations, alignments
  • Test-drive vehicles to confirm repairs worked
  • Explain needed repairs and costs to customers
  • Keep up with new vehicle technology, including hybrids and EVs

Where it can lead

  • Apprentice Technician
  • Licensed Automotive Service Technician (310S)
  • Senior Technician / Diagnostic Specialist
  • Service Advisor or Shop Foreperson
  • Shop Owner or Fleet Manager
