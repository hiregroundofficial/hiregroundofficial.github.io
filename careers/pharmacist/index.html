Pharmacist

Pharmacists make sure medications are safe and used correctly, they dispense prescriptions, check for dangerous drug interactions, counsel patients, and give vaccinations. About 17,350 pharmacists work in Ontario, mostly in community pharmacies and drug stores (76%) and hospitals (17%). Registration with the Ontario College of Pharmacists is required to practise.

What does a Pharmacist do?

Mainly community pharmacies and drug stores (76%), plus hospitals (17%). Work is fast-paced and detail-oriented, with constant patient interaction and standing for long periods.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Senior chemistry, biology, and math with strong grades. A part-time job in a pharmacy is a great way to see the work firsthand.

Undergraduate Studies

Most PharmD programs require at least 1–2 years of university prerequisites in sciences; many students complete a full bachelor's degree first.

Pharmacy Degree

Complete a Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD) from a CCAPP-accredited program (typically 4 years), including clinical placements.

Practical Training and Exams

Complete the approved Structured Practical Training (SPT) program, then pass the Pharmacy Examining Board of Canada (PEBC) Qualifying Exam and the OCP Jurisprudence Exam.

Entry-Level Position

Register with the Ontario College of Pharmacists and start as a staff pharmacist in a community pharmacy or hospital.

Career Progression

Become a pharmacy manager or owner, move into hospital/clinical pharmacy, specialize (e.g., geriatrics, oncology), or pursue research and teaching.

Licensing: Registration with the Ontario College of Pharmacists is required to practise in Ontario. This requires an accredited PharmD, structured practical training, the PEBC Qualifying Exam, and the College's jurisprudence exam.

Education time

6–7 years after high school

  • •Training: Structured Practical Training (SPT)
  • •Licensing: PEBC Qualifying Exam + OCP Jurisprudence Exam

Mandatory: The PharmD, SPT, PEBC exam, and OCP registration are mandatory.

Optional: A hospital pharmacy residency (1 year) is optional but needed for many hospital roles.

Job demand

Good

Employment growth will lead to several new positions, plus a moderate number of openings from retirements. Greater need for medication management for a growing, aging population is a key driver.

Stress level

Moderate

Moderate stress from fast-paced dispensing, high prescription volumes, the need for constant accuracy (medication errors can be dangerous), long periods of standing, and evening/weekend hours in retail pharmacies.

Work-life balance

Moderate

Retail pharmacy hours include evenings and weekends, but part-time work is common (16% part-time), and hospital pharmacists often have more regular schedules.

Flexibility

  • Remote workVery limited, dispensing and patient counselling require in-person presence.
  • SchedulingRetail pharmacies need evening and weekend coverage; part-time and casual shifts are widely available.
  • FreelanceSome locum and relief pharmacist work; 23% are self-employed, including pharmacy owners.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Verify and dispense prescription medications
  • Check for drug interactions, allergies, and dosing errors
  • Counsel patients on how to take medications and manage side effects
  • Administer vaccinations and give injections
  • Compound customized medications when needed
  • Collaborate with doctors and nurses on treatment plans
  • Manage inventory and order pharmaceutical supplies
  • Document prescriptions and patient records

Where it can lead

  • Staff pharmacist (community or hospital)
  • Senior pharmacist or pharmacy manager
  • Clinical/hospital pharmacist or specialist
  • Pharmacy owner
  • Research, teaching, or industry roles
