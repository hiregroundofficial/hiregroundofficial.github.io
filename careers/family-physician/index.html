Family Physician

Family physicians are the first doctor most people see, they diagnose and treat everyday illnesses, manage chronic conditions like diabetes, provide preventive care and vaccinations, and refer patients to specialists when needed. About 31,350 work in Ontario, and roughly two-thirds are self-employed. Demand is expected to be strong, especially in rural and northern communities.

What does a Family Physician do?

Mostly physicians' offices/clinics (62%) and hospitals (32%). Work involves direct patient contact, computer documentation, and collaboration with specialists and allied health professionals.

How to get there, step by step

The typical route into this career in Ontario. Required steps are marked, everything else is common or optional.

High School

Take senior biology, chemistry, physics, and English. Medical school admission is extremely competitive, strong grades and science preparation are essential. Volunteer in health care and build communication skills.

Undergraduate Degree

Complete a 4-year bachelor's degree (any major, but sciences are common) and write the MCAT admission test.

Medical School

Complete a 4-year Doctor of Medicine (MD) degree at an accredited medical school (3 years at McMaster University).

Residency

Complete a 2-year family medicine residency and pass the Medical Council of Canada qualifying exams.

Certification and Licensing

Become certified by the College of Family Physicians of Canada (CFPC) and obtain a licence from the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO).

Entry-Level Position

Practise as a family physician in a clinic, community health centre, hospital, or your own practice.

Career Progression

Build a patient roster, pursue enhanced-skills training (e.g., emergency medicine, sports medicine), take on teaching, or move into leadership or administration.

Job demand

Good

Employment growth will create several new positions, with a moderate number of openings from retirements. Aging population and health-system demand are key drivers; demand may be greater in rural and northern Ontario.

Stress level

High

High stress from large patient volumes, responsibility for diagnosis and treatment decisions, long and irregular hours, on-call duties, and heavy administrative workloads (charting, billing).

Work-life balance

Challenging

Long hours plus paperwork and on-call responsibilities strain balance, though some family physicians set part-time hours or choose clinic-based schedules with more control.

Flexibility

  • Remote workPartial, virtual visits are common for follow-ups, but most work is in-person clinical care.
  • SchedulingSome control over clinic hours if self-employed; hospital and walk-in roles have set shifts.
  • FreelanceVery common, 65% of family physicians in Ontario are self-employed. Locum (temporary) work is widely available.

What a typical day actually looks like

Concrete responsibilities, not one person's story, but the common core of the job.

  • Diagnose and treat common illnesses and injuries
  • Conduct physical examinations and take patient histories
  • Order and interpret blood tests, x-rays, and other diagnostics
  • Refer patients to specialists and coordinate their care
  • Provide vaccinations and preventive health screenings
  • Manage chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension
  • Counsel patients on lifestyle, mental health, and treatment options
  • Maintain detailed electronic medical records

Where it can lead

  • Family medicine resident
  • Staff family physician in a clinic or hospital
  • Practice owner or lead physician
  • Enhanced-skills physician (e.g., emergency, sports medicine)
  • Academic, administrative, or health-policy leadership roles
