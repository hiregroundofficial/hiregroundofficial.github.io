Creating a Resume

Your Highlight Reel

A resume is a highlight reel, not a biography.
Essential skill10 min read

Why Is a Resume Important?

A resume is a one to two page document that summarizes your education, skills, and experience so that an employer can quickly decide whether you are worth interviewing. It is often the very first impression you make, sometimes before anyone has met you.

A resume is your personal marketing tool. Employers may receive dozens or even hundreds of applications for one position, and they often spend only a short time on each resume. A clear, professional resume helps you stand out, shows that you are organized and detail-oriented, and highlights the skills that matter most for the job.

Key Terms

Resume
A short, targeted summary of your education, skills, and work experience, usually 1–2 pages long.
Curriculum Vitae (CV)
A longer, more detailed record of academic and professional history, mainly used for research, teaching, and academic positions.
Cover Letter
A short letter sent with your resume that explains why you are a good fit for a specific role.
Action Verb
A strong word that begins a bullet point and describes what you did (e.g., led, organized, designed).
Transferable Skills
Skills that apply to many jobs, such as communication, teamwork, and problem-solving.
Keywords
Important words and phrases from a job posting (skills, tools, qualifications) that employers look for in a resume.
ATS (Applicant Tracking System)
Software many employers use to scan, sort, and rank resumes before a person reads them.
Reference
A person, such as a former supervisor or teacher, who can vouch for your skills and character.

Resume vs. CV

In Canada, a resume is what most employers expect for jobs, co-ops, and internships. A CV is typically used for academic, research, medical, or scientific roles and can be several pages long. If a posting asks for a CV, follow their instructions.

Resume Formats

FormatDescriptionBest For
ChronologicalLists experience from most recent to oldest. The most common and most widely accepted format.People with steady work or volunteer history
FunctionalOrganizes information by skill categories instead of by job. De-emphasizes dates.Career changers or people with gaps (some employers dislike it)
CombinationStarts with a skills summary, then lists experience in chronological order.People with varied skills and some experience
Tip: If you are a student or have limited experience, the chronological or combination format usually works best.

What Goes on a Resume?

Most resumes include the following sections, in this general order:

  • Contact Information: Full name, phone number, professional email address, city and province, and optionally a LinkedIn link.
  • Summary or Objective (optional): 2–3 sentences describing who you are and what you offer.
  • Education: School, program, and expected or completed graduation date. Students can add relevant courses or awards.
  • Work Experience: Job title, employer, location, dates, and 3–4 bullet points describing your accomplishments.
  • Volunteer and Extracurricular Experience: Clubs, sports, community service, and leadership roles.
  • Skills: Technical skills (software, languages) and soft skills (teamwork, communication).
  • Awards and Certifications: Honours, scholarships, and certificates such as First Aid/CPR or WHMIS.

What Should NOT Be on a Resume?

  • Photos, age, marital status, or religion (not expected in Canada)
  • Your Social Insurance Number (SIN) or full home address
  • Unprofessional email addresses (e.g., coolgamer123@email.com)
  • Salary expectations or reasons for leaving past jobs
  • “References available upon request” (this is assumed)
  • False or exaggerated information, it can cost you the job later

Writing Strong Bullet Points

Bullet points are the heart of your resume. Weak bullets list duties, while strong bullets show results. A helpful formula is: Action Verb + What You Did + Result or Impact. Whenever possible, add numbers (quantify) to make your achievements concrete.

Weak BulletStrong Bullet
Responsible for serving customersServed 50+ customers per shift in a fast-paced cafe while maintaining a 100% accurate cash balance
Helped with the school clubOrganized 4 fundraising events as Club Treasurer, raising $1,200 for local charities
Worked on a group projectCollaborated with a team of 4 to design and present a marketing plan, earning the top grade in the class
Did social media postsCreated weekly Instagram content for a youth group, increasing followers by 35% in three months

Examples of Action Verbs

CategoryVerbs
Leadershipled, coordinated, managed, mentored, organized
Communicationpresented, wrote, negotiated, taught, promoted
Problem-Solvinganalyzed, improved, resolved, streamlined, developed

No experience? No problem! Everyone starts somewhere. If you have not had a job yet, include school projects, volunteering, sports, clubs, babysitting, tutoring, or personal projects. Employers of first-time workers want to see responsibility, reliability, and a willingness to learn.

Resumes and ATS

Many employers use an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) to filter resumes. These programs scan for keywords that match the job posting. To make your resume ATS-friendly:

  • Use standard section headings (Education, Work Experience, Skills)
  • Include keywords from the job posting, worded the same way
  • Use a simple layout: avoid text boxes, images, and complex tables
  • Choose a clean, readable font such as Arial, Calibri, or Times New Roman (10–12 pt)
  • Save and submit as a PDF or Word file, following the employer's instructions

How to Build a Resume

  1. Gather Your Information

    Start by making a “master list” of everything you have done: jobs, volunteer work, clubs, sports, awards, certifications, and skills. Include dates, locations, and responsibilities. This list is not your resume, but it is the raw material you will pull from every time you apply.

  2. Read the Job Posting

    Highlight the required skills, qualifications, and keywords in the posting. These tell you exactly what the employer wants to see.

  3. Choose a Format and Template

    Select a chronological or combination layout and a clean template (Google Docs, Word, or the Government of Canada's Job Bank Resume Builder all work well). Keep margins around 0.5–1 inch and use consistent fonts, spacing, and bullet styles.

  4. Write Your Sections

    Enter your contact information, education, and experience. Write 3–4 strong bullet points for each role using the Action Verb + Task + Result formula. Put the most relevant information first.

  5. Tailor It

    Adjust your resume for each application. Swap in keywords from the posting, reorder bullets so the most relevant come first, and remove anything that does not support the role. This is the most important part. A tailored resume is far more effective than a generic one.

  6. Proofread and Get Feedback

    Check for spelling, grammar, and formatting errors. Read it out loud, then ask a teacher, counsellor, parent, or mentor to review it. Save the final version as a PDF named professionally (e.g., FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf).

And repeat! Update your resume every few months or after any new job, award, or project so it is always ready.

Quick Checklist

  • Is it 1–2 pages (1 page for most students)?
  • Does it have a professional email and current contact information?
  • Does every bullet start with an action verb?
  • Did I include numbers and results where possible?
  • Does it include keywords from the job posting?
  • Is the formatting consistent and free of errors?
