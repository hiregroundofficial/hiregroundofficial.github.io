Career Planning

Mapping Your Path

A career is a road trip, not a teleport: plan the route, but stay open to detours.
Start here10 min read

Why Is Career Planning Important?

A career plan is your personal roadmap for the work you want to do, the skills you need to get there, and the steps you will take along the way. Many students feel pressure to have their whole future figured out, but that is not the goal. The goal is to make informed choices today that keep your options open tomorrow.

The choices you make now, which courses to take, which activities to join, which programs to apply to, shape the doors that are open to you later. Planning helps you spend your time and money wisely, avoid programs that are not a good fit, and discover options you may not have known existed. It is also motivating: it is much easier to work through a difficult class when you can see how it connects to something you care about.

Key Terms

Career
The series of jobs, learning experiences, volunteer roles, and responsibilities that make up your working life. A career is bigger than any single job.
Career Path
The route you take toward a career, including education, training, work experience, and a few unexpected turns.
Values
The things that matter most to you in work and life, such as helping others, stability, creativity, independence, or income.
Strengths
Skills and qualities you are naturally good at or have developed through practice.
SMART Goal
A goal that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound.
NOC (National Occupational Classification)
Canada's national system for organizing and describing occupations, using five-digit codes.
TEER Category
A number from 0 to 5 in the NOC that shows the usual training, education, experience, and responsibilities needed for an occupation.
Labour Market Information (LMI)
Data about jobs in Canada, such as wages, job openings, and how much demand there is for a type of worker.
Job Shadowing
Spending a day or part of a day following a professional at work to see what the job is really like.
Mentor
A more experienced person who offers guidance, advice, and support as you grow.
Plan B
A backup route toward your goal, or toward a related goal, in case your first choice does not work out.

Know Yourself First

The best career decisions begin with self-knowledge. Before you research jobs, take time to reflect on who you are and what you want from work.

AreaQuestions to Ask YourselfExamples
InterestsWhat do I enjoy doing in my free time? Which classes do I look forward to?Building things, animals, gaming, art, sports, helping people
StrengthsWhat do people ask me for help with? What comes easily to me?Problem-solving, public speaking, organizing, drawing, math
ValuesWhat do I want my work to give me? What would make a job feel worthwhile?Helping others, security, flexibility, creativity, teamwork
Work StyleDo I prefer working alone or with others? Indoors or outdoors?Hands-on work, desk work, leading a team
Tip: Online interest and personality quizzes can be a fun starting point for ideas, but they are not a verdict on your future. Use them to generate questions, then test the ideas in real life.

Understanding Your Pathways

After high school, there is more than one way to build a career. In Ontario, the four main pathways are apprenticeship, college, university, and the workplace, and many people combine or switch between them over time. Every pathway is valid; the right one depends on your goals, learning style, and interests.

PathwayDescriptionBest For
ApprenticeshipCombines paid, on-the-job training with in-class learning to earn a trade certification.Hands-on learners who want to earn while they learn
CollegePrograms of about one to three years that focus on practical, job-ready skills, often with work placements.People who want applied training for a specific career
UniversityDegree programs (bachelor's and beyond) that focus on theory, research, and critical thinking.Careers that require a degree, or people who enjoy in-depth study
WorkplaceEntering the workforce directly and learning through on-the-job training and experience.People ready to start working or who want experience first
Tip: Many Ontario students build an Individual Pathways Plan (IPP) at school and can take co-operative education (co-op) placements to try out a field. Ask your guidance counsellor what is available at your school, since programs and requirements can change.

Exploring Careers

Once you know yourself better, it is time to explore. Aim to look at a range of careers, including ones you have never heard of.

  • Online research: The Government of Canada's Job Bank lets you look up occupations to see typical duties, wages, education requirements, and job outlook.
  • Talking to people: Ask family, teachers, coaches, and community members about their work, or request an informational interview through LinkedIn.
  • Job shadowing and co-op: Spend time inside a real workplace to see what a typical day feels like.
  • Volunteering, part-time work, and clubs: Try roles and activities that connect to your interests and build transferable skills at the same time.

Reading the Job Market

Labour market information helps you make choices based on facts as well as passion. In Canada, occupations are organized by the National Occupational Classification (NOC), and each one has a TEER category showing the usual level of training and education required.

TEERUsually RequiresExample Occupations
TEER 0Management experience and responsibilitiesFinancial managers; marketing managers
TEER 1A university degreeFinancial advisors; software engineers
TEER 2A college diploma or apprenticeship of two or more years, or supervisory dutiesMedical laboratory technologists; web technicians
TEER 3A college diploma or apprenticeship of under two years, or more than six months of on-the-job trainingBakers and pastry chefs; dental assistants
TEER 4A high school diploma, or several weeks of on-the-job trainingRetail salespersons; babysitters
TEER 5A short work demonstration and no formal educationLandscaping labourers; delivery drivers

When you research an occupation, look for what the work involves, the education needed, typical wages, and the job outlook (whether demand is expected to grow, stay steady, or shrink). Wages and outlook vary by region and change over time.

Setting SMART Goals

SMART goals turn big ideas into clear steps. Test every goal against the five letters below.

LetterWhat to AskExample
S, SpecificWhat exactly do I want to accomplish?Learn what a nurse does daily
M, MeasurableHow will I know I have achieved it?Complete 20 volunteer hours
A, AchievableIs this realistic with my time and resources?Volunteer two hours a week
R, RelevantDoes it connect to my bigger career goals?Tests a health care career
T, Time-boundWhat is my deadline?Finish by the end of the school year

Here is how weak goals compare with SMART goals:

Weak GoalSMART Goal
Get better at businessJoin the school business club and complete one case competition by the end of Semester 2
Look into college programsResearch five college programs in my area of interest and attend two open houses by December
Get some work experienceApply to at least six part-time jobs and complete a co-op placement or 30 volunteer hours this year

Have a Plan B (and a Plan C)

Even great plans hit obstacles: a program may be full, a job may not be available, or your interests may change. That is normal. A Plan B is not a sign of failure, it is a smart way to stay flexible. Your backup might be a related program, a different pathway to the same career, or a first job that builds skills for the one you really want.

How to Build Your Career Plan

  1. Reflect on Yourself

    Write down your interests, strengths, values, and preferred work style. Ask two or three people who know you well what they think your strengths are. Notice which activities make time fly.

  2. Brainstorm Options

    List 8–10 careers or fields that connect to your reflections, even ones that seem unlikely. Then use Job Bank and the NOC to narrow the list to your top three to five.

  3. Research and Test Your Options

    For each top choice, look up duties, education requirements, wages, and job outlook. Then get real experience through job shadowing, co-op, volunteering, part-time work, or an informational interview. This is the most important part. Reading about a job is very different from seeing it up close, trying things is the fastest way to learn what fits.

  4. Choose a Pathway and Set SMART Goals

    Decide which pathway or pathways best support your top choices. Write two or three short-term goals (this semester) and one or two long-term goals (three to five years), and make each one SMART.

  5. Create an Action Plan

    List the courses, skills, activities, and deadlines you need to meet your goals, such as program application dates. Add a Plan B, and share your plan with a counsellor, teacher, or mentor for feedback.

  6. Review and Adjust

    Check your plan at least twice a year. Celebrate what you have completed, remove what no longer fits, and update your resume and LinkedIn profile with new experiences.

And repeat! Your interests and the job market will both change over time. Revisit your plan at the start of each school year and whenever you reach a milestone, so it always reflects who you are now.

Quick Checklist

  • Have I reflected on my interests, strengths, values, and work style?
  • Have I explored at least three careers using reliable sources like Job Bank?
  • Have I tried at least one hands-on experience, such as job shadowing, volunteering, or an informational interview?
  • Do I understand the pathways (apprenticeship, college, university, workplace) that lead to my top choices?
  • Are my goals SMART, with clear deadlines?
  • Do I have a Plan B and someone I can ask for advice?
  • Do I have a date on my calendar to review my plan?
