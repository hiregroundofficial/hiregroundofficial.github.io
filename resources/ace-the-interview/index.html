Ace the Interview

Answering Questions with Confidence

Confidence comes from preparation, not from luck.
Essential skill10 min read

Why Is Interview Preparation Important?

An interview is your chance to turn a piece of paper into a person. Your resume got you in the door, but the interview is where an employer decides whether you are someone they want on their team. Many people feel nervous, and that is completely normal.

Employers use interviews to learn things a resume cannot show: how you communicate, how you think under pressure, and whether you would work well with others. Two candidates with identical resumes can have very different outcomes based solely on how they answer questions. The good news: interviews are more predictable than they seem, and the people who perform best are usually not the most naturally talented speakers, but the ones who practiced.

Key Terms

Interview
A conversation, in person, by phone, or online, in which an employer learns about your skills, experience, and personality to decide if you are a good fit.
Behavioural Question
A question that asks about a past situation (e.g., “Tell me about a time when…”) because past behaviour predicts future performance.
STAR Method
A framework for answering behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result.
Elevator Pitch
A short (30–60 second) summary of who you are and what you offer.
Panel Interview
An interview where you meet with several interviewers at the same time.
Follow-Up
A thank-you message sent after the interview to show appreciation and reinforce your interest.
Body Language
Non-verbal communication such as posture, eye contact, and facial expressions.

Answering with the STAR Method

Behavioural questions are very common, especially for students and first-time job seekers. The STAR method helps you tell a clear, complete story in about 1–2 minutes:

LetterWhat to Say
S: SituationSet the scene. Where were you and what was happening?
T: TaskWhat was your responsibility or goal?
A: ActionWhat did YOU do? Focus on your own steps (use “I,” not just “we”).
R: ResultWhat happened? Include numbers or lessons learned when possible.

STAR Example

Question: “Tell me about a time you worked in a team.”
Situation: In Grade 11, my group was assigned a science presentation, but two members were struggling to agree on a topic.
Task: As group lead, I needed to get everyone aligned before the deadline.
Action: I organized a short meeting, had each person share one idea, and helped the group vote. I then split tasks based on everyone's strengths.
Result: We finished two days early and received a 92%. I learned that listening first makes teamwork easier.

Common Interview Questions

“Tell me about yourself” is usually the first question, and it is not an invitation to share your life story. Use the Present → Past → Future structure: start with who you are now (your school or current role), briefly mention relevant past experience or skills, and finish with why you are excited about this job. Keep it to about one minute.

QuestionWhat They Are Really AskingHow to Answer
Why do you want to work here?Did you research us? Are you genuinely interested?Mention specific things about the company (values, products, reputation) and link them to your goals.
What are your strengths?What can you offer us?Choose 2–3 strengths relevant to the job and support each with a short example.
What is your weakness?Are you self-aware and willing to grow?Name a real, minor weakness and explain the steps you are taking to improve it.
Tell me about a time you faced a challenge.How do you handle problems?Use the STAR method and highlight what you learned.
Where do you see yourself in 5 years?Are you ambitious and likely to stay?Share realistic goals for learning and growth that connect to the role.
Why should we hire you?What makes you the best choice?Summarize your top skills, your enthusiasm, and how you can help the team.

Questions You Should Ask

At the end of most interviews, you will be asked, “Do you have any questions for us?” Always say yes! Good questions show genuine interest. Try:

  • “What does a typical day or week look like in this role?”
  • “What qualities do your most successful employees share?”
  • “What are the next steps in the hiring process?”
Tip: Avoid asking about pay, vacation, or perks in a first interview unless the interviewer brings it up.

Know Your Rights (Canada)

Under Canadian human rights laws, employers generally should not ask about protected topics such as age, religion, race, marital or family status, or disability during an interview. If you are asked something inappropriate, you can politely redirect, for example: “I'd be happy to talk about my availability and how I can meet the needs of this role.” Rules vary slightly by province, so check your provincial human rights commission for details.

Body Language and Presentation

  • Dress: Choose clean, professional clothing that fits the workplace. When unsure, dress one step above the company's usual style.
  • Greeting: Smile, make eye contact, and offer a firm (not crushing) handshake if appropriate.
  • Posture: Sit up straight, avoid crossing your arms, and keep your hands relaxed.
  • Voice: Speak clearly and at a steady pace. Pausing to think for a few seconds is perfectly fine.
  • Phone: Silence it and put it away before you walk in.

How to Prepare for an Interview

  1. Research the Company and Role

    Read the job posting and the company's website and social media. Learn what they do, their values, and recent news. Highlight the skills they want most.

  2. Match Your Experience

    For each key skill in the posting, think of a real example from school, work, volunteering, or activities. Write them down in STAR format.

  3. Practice Out Loud

    Answer common questions out loud, not just in your head. Practice with a friend, family member, or teacher, or record yourself to spot filler words such as “um” and “like.” This is the most important part. Confidence comes from repetition.

  4. Prepare Your Materials

    Pack extra copies of your resume, a list of references, a pen, a notepad, and any required ID. For virtual interviews, test your technology the day before.

  5. Show Up and Perform

    Arrive early, greet everyone politely, listen carefully to each question, and pause before answering. If you do not understand a question, ask for clarification. If you do not know an answer, be honest and explain how you would figure it out.

  6. Follow Up

    Within 24 hours, send a short thank-you email. Thank the interviewer for their time, mention something specific you enjoyed discussing, and restate your interest in the role.

And repeat! Every interview, whether you get the job or not, is practice. Afterward, write down which questions were hardest and improve your answers for next time.

Quick Checklist

  • Did I research the company and reread the job posting?
  • Do I have 4–5 STAR stories ready?
  • Can I answer “Tell me about yourself” in about one minute?
  • Do I have 2–3 thoughtful questions to ask?
  • Are my outfit, materials, and technology ready?
  • Have I planned to send a thank-you message?
